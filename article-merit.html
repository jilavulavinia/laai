Meritocracy in the times of AI: feeling useless is the new norm

When the skills that once earned people merit can be reproduced by anyone with a subscription, what happens to their sense of worth?

Written by
Lavinia Lorena Jilavu
Published
2 November 2026
Reading time
4 min

Your skills feel useless

Open LinkedIn this morning and the feed reads like a eulogy for the knowledge worker. Between the AI hype posts sit the announcements: roles cut because a company decided an AI agent could do the same job faster and, obviously, cheaper. Companies like Canva laying off 22% of their workforce are just one indicator. If you have scrolled past those posts and felt a small knot in your stomach, you are not alone. The more interesting question is how we got here.

Modern societies run on a simple contract: work hard, build skills, and you will be rewarded and respected. We call it meritocracy, and it quietly ties a person’s worth to what they can do better than others. Generative AI tears up the small print. Large language models and their multimodal successors can now write, draw, code and analyse in seconds what took years of education and thousands spent on degrees and courses. When skill can be cloned on demand, the link between effort, skill and recognition starts to simply break.

This research asks an uncomfortable question: what happens to people’s sense of worth when the skills that once earned them merit can be reproduced by anyone with a subscription? The working hypothesis is deliberately provocative: feeling useless is becoming the new normal at work. Not because people are losing their jobs, though some are, but because they are losing the sense that their contribution matters.

Intelligence + effort = merit

Michael Young boiled meritocracy down to one equation: IQ + effort = merit. Neat, fair and effective, it could sort any job market. Then AI broke the maths. Suddenly the effort looks optional, the intelligence can be rented by the month, and people start to feel threatened by, and even resentful of, the very work they trained for.

Young coined the term in 1958, and he meant it as a warning: a society in which individuals advance and are rewarded for their skills, achievements and hard work, rather than their social status or background (Drishti IAS).

But we are not here for history lessons. We are here to learn what is happening right now in the market, and right now merit feels slippery. We might feel our work is simply checking an LLM’s output. And one might say: that is not what I signed up for. That makes sense. AI added a twist: human-centred definitions of merit, and access to AI tools, may start to feel more relevant than pure knowledge (Drishti IAS). AI makes the question sharper: what is left to be proud of when the machine did most of the work?

What the evidence says so far

Workers are more anxious than hopeful.

Quite incredible how, not even five years ago, working at a big tech firm was the dream, and now that same dream is working for one of the (maybe) ten AI firms. The shift in society is visible, and competition is absolutely ravenous. LinkedIn ranks AI engineer as the fastest-growing job title, and ManpowerGroup reports that 76% of employers say they can’t find the AI talent they need (Acceler8 Talent).

So you might not be entirely useless. It depends on whether you keep authorship of your work, or become the operator of a machine.
