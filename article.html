AI in AEC: From hype to actual work

Adoption is growing fast, but most firms can’t show what AI has earned them. The return starts long before the first prompt.

Written by
Lavinia Lorena Jilavu
Published
5 November 2026
Reading time
6 min

AEC as fertile ground for AI

Architecture, engineering and construction is one of the largest industries in the world, and it still runs on a surprising amount of manual work. There are clear settings which cannot be run with AI, but the majority of the early phase design, up to the permit drawings phase, can.

We are looking at an incredible opportunity for AI development, the second biggest industry in the world. The market is worth between $11 and $16 trillion: a report from ResearchAndMarkets puts it at $15.78 trillion in 2024, rising to $16.45 trillion in 2025. Deloitte’s more conservative figure is $11.39 trillion in 2024, growing to $16.11 trillion by 2030 (Deloitte).

However, half of the market margin is covered by 7 of the biggest companies in the world, dominating the entire scene. This is why AI application in AEC is fairly hard to document, and extrapolating data is essential if we want to look at it with the correct lens.

Easier said than done for small and mid-sized firms

Most of the sector does not look like Gensler or Arup. It is made up of small and medium practices that are only partly digitalized. These firms often use BIM on some projects, choose their tools on a “cheap to expensive” rating, receive documentation in PDF, don’t have an automatic system for internal checking, and use spreadsheets and email for everything else. For them, bringing AI in efficiently, safely and ethically is a real challenge.

The size gap shows up clearly in the data: large practices use AI far more than small ones, and few practices of any size have invested in governing it.

3 of the many whys

Adoption is growing fast: the share of UK architecture practices using AI rose from 41% in 2024 to 59% in 2025 (RIBA). So why does it still stall for so many firms?

  • Fear of the transition. A 2025 RICS survey found that 2 in 5 construction professionals worry about AI’s impact on their own roles, and that accuracy of AI outputs is their main concern (ForConstructionPros). In architecture specifically, over two-thirds (67%) worry that AI will increase the risk of their work being imitated (RIBA).
  • Economics and risk. Firms that haven’t adopted AI cite risk, cost and integration challenges (Bluebeam). More than 40% name data-sharing security and IP risk as a barrier. Regulation adds to the uncertainty: 69% of respondents saw possible AI regulations as a major challenge, a point that lands especially well for a European audience facing the EU AI Act.
  • “I don’t know where to start.” This may be the biggest barrier. In BST Global’s survey, 91% named identifying or proving use cases with business value as a top barrier, and only about 20% considered themselves at a mature or advanced level (ENR). RICS found that 45% of firms have no AI implementation at all and another 34% are still running early pilots. Skills are part of the problem too: 32% of firms report a lack of training and skills development as a key challenge (HPAC).

The prompt-and-pray AI

That lack of training shows up in daily work. Most AEC firms using AI have never been taught how to use it, so the method is kinda simple: type a prompt, cross your fingers, wait for magic.

We’ve all been there. A tender lands on your desk, the deadline is tomorrow, and you think: this is exactly what AI is for. So you open Claude and type, “Hey, I have this tender to analyze, can you do it for me?” Thirty seconds later, Claude hands back a flawless analysis that catches every clause and every risk, and you take the afternoon off.

Nah. That’s not what happens, is it?

What you usually get is a tidy little summary, shorter than you hoped and lighter on detail than you needed. Not wrong, exactly. Just not what you had in mind.

That’s the tricky part of working with AI: ask vaguely, and it will answer confidently, just not the question you meant.

But is this really all AI can deliver? Or are there many more layers to consider before saying “X firm is adopting AI”?

The ROI paradox

AI can amplify almost everything a firm does, and today’s models can take in more information than ever before. Companies are betting heavily on it: 85% of organizations raised their AI spending last year, yet only 6% saw a measurable return within twelve months (Deloitte, October 2025, via HSO).

AEC is no exception, because using AI and getting value from it are two different things. In Unanet’s 2026 AEC Inspire Report, 75% of roughly 300 U.S. AEC leaders said they use AI, but only 29% had high confidence in the data behind it (via Advisor AI Solutions).

So what is the AI ROI paradox in AEC? Put simply, firms are spending on AI without the numbers to prove it pays off. Few can say how much AI has saved them in financial terms, and fewer still can trace a business decision back to it and measure the outcome.

The firms that do see returns tend to start with a clear use case. Among AI users in a 2025 Bluebeam survey, 68% said they had saved at least $50,000, and nearly half had saved 500–1,000 hours (Bluebeam).

To close, the real return on AI starts long before the first prompt, with the unglamorous work of structuring, cleaning and connecting data. A well-structured BIM model is exactly that kind of data source, which makes BIM maturity a prerequisite for AI in AEC. But data alone is not enough: firms also need proper governance of their AI systems, not just a prompt-and-pray approach.
