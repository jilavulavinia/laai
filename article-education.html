AI and architectural education: a paradigm shift

For the first time, the tool in the studio proposes, explains and responds to critique. Students have already moved. Schools now have to decide what kind of architect they want to produce.

Written by
Lavinia Lorena Jilavu
Published
5 November 2026
Reading time
6 min

Architectural education has absorbed new tools before. In the late 90s traditional drawing gave way to CAD, and CAD then led to BIM and parametric modelling. Each time, the tools changed quite easily, while the courses themselves stayed much the same.

Why this is a paradigm shift

The student designed, the tool executed, and the professor judged. AI breaks that pattern. For the first time, the tool does more than execute: it proposes, explains its choices and responds to critique. Many professors might find this intimidating, and the data suggests many do.

In a 2025 College Board survey of more than 3,000 U.S. college faculty across all disciplines, 45% held a negative view of AI in higher education against 34% positive, and over 84% agreed that it reduces students’ critical thinking, originality and engagement (College Board).

On academic integrity, 92% were concerned about plagiarism or dishonesty made easier by AI. Yet this debate frames AI almost entirely as a writing shortcut. What if it were also used to enhance collaborative research? So far, academic use of AI has centred on one of its core functions: automation, in which you, the user, issue an instruction and the tool carries it out. Its capacity for augmentation, extending knowledge through an ethical, collaborative partnership between student and AI, remains largely unexamined and, in my opinion, rarely assessed.

Randy Deutsch, who has taught architecture studios for 25 years, describes how his 2026 AI studio forced him to rethink the roles of student, professor and tool, and even where authority sits in the studio (Common Edge).

That is what makes this a paradigm shift rather than another software update. The question for schools is no longer whether to teach AI, but what kind of architect they want to produce: one who is fluent with the tools, or one who can judge what the tools produce.

Where things stand

Quite frankly, students have already moved. Across higher education, AI use is close to universal. In the UK, HEPI’s 2025 survey of 1,041 undergraduates found that 92% use AI in some form, up from 66% a year earlier, and 88% use it for assessments (HEPI). In the US, Inside Higher Ed’s 2025 Student Voice survey found that 85% of students had used AI for coursework in the past year (eWeek).

Students feel that institutions are not supporting this transition. Only 29% of UK students say their university encourages AI use, while 40% say it does not (HEPI). Students want guidance rather than bans: 53% ask for education on ethical AI use and 51% for clear, standardised rules (eWeek). Often the answers they look for in a professor are found in an AI agent instead, which wrongly becomes the perfect persona to replace the institutional figure.

The tensions

Deutsch’s account of his AI studio at the Brooklyn Marine Terminal is one of the most detailed from inside a classroom, and it covers most of the open questions (Common Edge).

  • Authorship. If an AI generated the massing, is it still the student’s design? His students argued it was, because the outputs came from their own iterative prompting and they treated every output as an editable starting point rather than a finished result. However, one of the most important uncertainties surfaces here: what is the material that AI generates actually based on? The provenance of AI outputs can never be fully traced, and nobody yet knows whose aesthetics, histories and ideas sit inside the training data.
  • The professor’s role. Deutsch argues that the professor’s informational authority, knowing more precedents and technical details than the student, is permanently reduced. What remains is the professor’s judgment: reading the student rather than the project, spotting where their thinking gets stuck, and pushing them further.
  • Assessment and accreditation. Traditional studio grading rewards the final outcome. Deutsch suggests assessing judgment, process and critical reflection first. The NAAB wrote its performance criteria before generative AI existed, which leaves a question mark over AI-assisted work: does it show student competence or tool competence?

This is where the perfect AI persona, the one that always answers, whether it’s 3 am or 7 pm, never disagreeing with the student and never questioning their judgment, becomes a quite dangerous adversary.

Toward a new paradigm

Putting the evidence together, the shift looks something like this:

From a tool that executes to a partner that critiques
RoleThe old paradigmThe emerging paradigm
The toolExecutes what the student drawsProposes, explains and responds to critique
The professorSource of precedents, technique and judgmentCoach of judgment, process and critical thinking
The studentAuthor and producer of drawingsAuthor, curator and director of outputs
What is assessedMainly the final outcomeJudgment, process and reflection, then outcome
How AI is learnedInformally, through social media and peersPart of the curriculum, with ethics and limits

What schools could do now

  1. Teach AI literacy, not just AI tools. Follow the SCOSA recommendation: students should understand what a model optimises for, what it flattens, and where its outputs come from.
  2. Make the process visible. Ask students to document prompts, iterations and edits, so reviewers can see where the student’s judgment shaped the result.
  3. Rewrite assessment criteria. Grade the ability to question, curate and reject outputs, not only the polish of the final images.
  4. Train the teachers. Close the student–faculty gap with workshops, so tutors can critique AI-assisted work with confidence.
  5. Set clear, fair rules. Publish a school-wide AI policy, and provide shared access to paid tools so the studio does not split by budget.
  6. Keep the studio human. Protect the desk crit, peer review and productive frustration, the parts of studio learning no AI can replace yet.
