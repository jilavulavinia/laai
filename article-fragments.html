The Missing Middle: why AEC AI is built in fragments

An overview of The state of AI in 2026 (McKinsey), and why AI in architecture, engineering and construction is being built as a set of islands.

Written by
Lavinia Lorena Jilavu
Published
11 November 2026
Reading time
6 min

A set of islands

AI in architecture, engineering and construction is being built as a set of islands: one tool for massing, another for code checks, another for takeoffs, another for site progress. Each island makes a single task faster. Very few of them talk to each other, and the project, which is where AEC actually makes or loses money, falls through the gaps between them.

McKinsey’s The state of AI in 2026: On the road to ROI (August 2026) describes the same pattern at the scale of the whole economy. Eight in ten respondents say AI has improved their own productivity, yet the share reporting any EBIT impact is stuck at 37 percent, unchanged from 2025 (pp. 11–12). Individual gains are real; organizational gains are not following.

What the survey says about AEC

Engineering and construction is adopting AI, but more slowly and more narrowly than the economy as a whole. Its strongest agent use is in software engineering (8 percent scaling) and IT (7 percent); the functions that define a building project, such as product development, supply chain and strategy, sit between 1 and 5 percent (sidebar exhibit, p. 8).

On the other measures, E&C sits close to the average, which matters: the sector is spending and experimenting, it is just not yet connecting.

The pattern is consistent: comparable intent and budget, lower depth. AEC firms are buying and trying AI, but few have pushed it to the scaling phase in the functions where projects are actually designed and delivered.

The fragmentation map

AEC AI tools cluster around single, well-defined tasks inside one project phase, and almost none own the handoff to the next phase. That is where projects lose time, money and design intent.

Each island can be honestly perfectly functional on its own and even independent. The problem is the dashed layer underneath: there is no common model the tools share, so every phase change means exporting, re-entering and re-checking. The value an AI tool creates in one phase is partly lost before it reaches the next.

Why AEC AI companies stay narrow

Point solutions are not a failure of imagination. They are the rational response to how AEC is organized, how its data is stored, and how AI startups are funded. Seven forces push vendors toward single tasks.

  1. The buyer is fragmented before the software is. A building is delivered by an owner, an architect, several engineering consultants, a general contractor and dozens of subcontractors, each under a separate contract. Each is a different customer with a different budget. A tool that spans phases has no single buyer, so vendors sell to whoever owns one step.
  2. There is no shared data layer. Design intent lives in proprietary authoring files, cost in spreadsheets, schedules in planning tools, site reality in photos and daily logs. Open standards such as IFC exist but rarely carry the full meaning across a handoff. An AI that wants to reason across phases first has to rebuild the project’s data model, which is a much larger company than an AI feature.
  3. Project data dies at handover. Most AEC data is organized per project, not per firm. When a project closes, its context is archived rather than reused, so there is little cross-project history for a broad system to learn from.
  4. Liability rewards narrow scope. Professional responsibility sits with licensed people. A tool that flags code issues on one sheet is easy to verify and easy to defend. An agent that changes design, cost and schedule together creates questions of accountability that no contract template answers yet.
  5. Venture economics rewards a wedge. A startup needs a measurable before-and-after in months, not years. “Takeoffs in two hours instead of two days” sells; “better projects” does not. The survey shows that even large companies now measure AI impact function by function (p. 12, note to Exhibit 6), which reinforces task-level selling.
  6. Incumbent platforms own the ground. The authoring and common-data environments belong to a handful of established vendors. Startups build plugins and add-ons inside those ecosystems, which limits them to what the host exposes.
  7. Long agentic chains are expensive and fragile. Every extra step in a multi-step workflow adds tokens, latency and a chance of error. McKinsey’s commentary notes that per-token prices fell while token consumption rose even faster (p. 17). A narrow tool keeps both cost and error rates contained.

None of these forces is unique to AEC, but AEC has all seven at once. That is why the market looks like a map of islands.

What fragmentation costs

Fragmentation caps AEC’s AI gains at the task level, which is exactly where the survey says most organizations are stuck. The report’s own diagnosis of who escapes that trap reads like a description of what AEC’s tool market cannot currently offer.

  • High performers redesign workflows; others insert AI into them. Nearly three-quarters of AI high performers say they have fundamentally redesigned workflows because of AI, up from 55 percent a year earlier. Only about one-quarter of other respondents say the same (p. 18). A point tool can only be inserted. Redesigning the path from concept to permit requires the steps to share context.
  • High performers invest in a connected data layer. One of the practices McKinsey tracks is having a semantic layer or knowledge graph that organizes disparate data sources into coherent objects and links. High performers report it far more often than others (p. 19, Exhibit 11). In AEC terms, that is a project model that design, cost, compliance and site tools all read from and write to.
  • High performers pursue growth and innovation, not only efficiency. About 80 percent of both groups chase efficiency, but 74 percent of high performers also target growth, against 47 percent of others (p. 18, Exhibit 10). Task tools are efficiency tools. New services, such as guaranteed permitting timelines or cost certainty at concept stage, need cross-phase capability.

The practical effect for an AEC firm: each tool saves hours inside its step, while the rework, re-entry and coordination between steps stay manual. In AEC, the change that matters most is connecting the steps. Time saved in drafting is lost in the next model exchange. The firm sees busier, more productive people and an unchanged project margin, which is the survey’s headline paradox in miniature.
